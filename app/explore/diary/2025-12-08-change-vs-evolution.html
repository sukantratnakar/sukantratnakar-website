Change Can Be Reversed. Evolution Can't.

change-management leadership evolution

Some conversations change the way ideas move. My chat with Jolene Zidkovich did that for me — the kind where the kinetic energy of ideas suddenly accelerated.

We were talking about something leaders struggle with all the time: the difference between change and evolution.

Change is a simple shift from point A to point B.
Evolution is a continuous journey made of many such shifts, where each step aligns with a larger direction.

In management research, this distinction is clear. Organizations evolve when their internal changes match the pace and direction of their environment — what scholars call "environmental velocity." When a change fits that larger curve, it becomes meaningful and long-term. When it doesn't, it's just movement.

The recent Airbus incident is a real-world example.

In late 2025, Airbus discovered that intense solar radiation could corrupt certain flight-control data on A320-family jets. Almost 6,000 aircraft worldwide were asked to undergo a precautionary update. To act quickly, regulators and Airbus rolled back a previous software update until a corrected version is ready.

That rollback is still a change — but it's a reverse change. It restores safety, but it doesn't move the system forward.

Evolution didn't stop, but it did take a detour. The real evolution will happen when the corrected software is released, tested against solar-radiation scenarios, and adopted across the fleet. That's when the trajectory continues.

And that's the deeper lesson:
Change can be reversed.
Evolution can't.

Not every change is progress. Only those shifts that align with the larger evolution curve of the industry, technology, or company truly matter.

For leaders, the question is simple:
Is this change moving us forward — or just moving us?
